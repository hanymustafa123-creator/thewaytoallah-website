Introduction to the Course

The Arabic Alphabet & Writing System

الحروف الهجائية العربية ونظام الكتابة
Before beginning the grammar lessons, it is essential to understand how Arabic is written and how its sound system works. This introduction follows Alan Jones's own prefatory material faithfully.

About This Book and Course

Arabic Through the Qur'an was written by Alan Jones, Emeritus Professor of Classical Arabic at the University of Oxford. It is one of the most respected works designed to take English-speaking learners directly to the Arabic of the Qur'an, without detours through Modern Standard Arabic.

The Book's Central Aim: To enable the reader to learn enough Arabic to be able to read the Qur'an in its original language. In its 40 lessons the book covers all the important points of the grammar of Qur'anic Arabic — though not every single point, since some problems of Qur'anic grammar the grammarians, Arab and non-Arab alike, have never fully resolved.

How This Course Is Structured

Each lesson in the original book consists of three parts:

(a) Grammar exposition — Each grammatical point is explained and illustrated with Qur'anic examples. Each piece of grammar should be mastered before moving on to the next.

(b) Vocabulary — Every lesson has its own vocabulary list. All words needed for the examples and exercises are included. You must learn each lesson's vocabulary thoroughly before attempting the exercise.

(c) The Exercise — All 40 exercises consist entirely of Qur'anic quotations — real verses that illustrate the grammar of that lesson.

How to Approach Vocabulary Learning

Jones stresses this point repeatedly: with nouns (and adjectives) you must learn the singular and the plural together. With verbs, you must learn three forms together — the Perfect, the Imperfect, and the Verbal Noun (Maṣdar) — even from the very first verb lesson. Do not skip this discipline; it pays great dividends later.

The Qur'anic Text Used

The text of the Qur'an referred to throughout this book is the Egyptian Standard Edition, first issued in 1342/1923 and revised in 1381/1960. This is the edition universally used in Qur'anic teaching worldwide. It is fully vocalised — all short vowels are written — which makes it the ideal text for learning.

Most Arabic texts — newspapers, books, signs — are written without short vowels. The fully-vocalised Qur'an is therefore both your primary learning tool and your greatest advantage as a beginner.

The Arabic Writing System

Arabic is written from right to left in a script called Naskh — the standard printed form. The alphabet has 29 letters (or 28 if one counts strictly, as Jones notes — see the Hamza section). Every letter is a consonant; vowels are added as separate marks above or below the letters.

Fundamental Differences from English Script
  • Arabic is written right to left
  • Most letters connect to the letter before and after them
  • Each letter has up to four forms: isolated, initial, medial, and final
  • Short vowels are normally not written in everyday Arabic — but they ARE written in the Qur'an (fully vocalised)
  • The alphabet is entirely consonants; vowel markers are added signs

History of the Script

The Arabic script developed significantly after the time of the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ. Early Qur'anic manuscripts (Kufic script) used few or no dots to differentiate letters. By the end of the first Islamic century, determined efforts were made to differentiate letters fully through dots. At the same time, the system for writing short vowels (ḥarakāt) was introduced, so the full text of the Qur'an could be recited correctly even by those unfamiliar with the Arabic dialect of the revelation.

The Naskh Script

The form of Arabic you will learn to read is Naskh — the primary printed form. It has fewer ligatures (connected letter-combinations) than other scripts like Thuluth or Ruqʿa. One ligature is so common it has almost become a letter: the combination of Lām + Alif, written as لا (lā).

Sun Letters and Moon Letters

This is one of the first and most important rules of Arabic pronunciation. When the definite article الـ (al-) is prefixed to a word, what happens to the ل (lām) depends on the first letter of the word:

☀️ Sun Letters — 14 letters

When al- precedes these letters, the lām is assimilated (doubles to match the following letter). These letters are:

ت ث د ذ ر ز س ش ص ض ط ظ ل ن

Example: الشَّمْسُ — al-shamsu → pronounced ash-shamsu (the sun). A shadda appears over the first letter of the word to show doubling.

🌙 Moon Letters — 15 letters

When al- precedes these letters, the lām is pronounced clearly as l. These letters are:

أ ب ج ح خ ع غ ف ق ك م و ه ي

Example: الْقَمَرُ — al-qamaru (the moon). A sukūn appears over the lām showing it is pronounced.

The names "sun letters" (حروف شمسية) and "moon letters" (حروف قمرية) come from the fact that the word for "sun" (شمس) begins with a sun letter, and the word for "moon" (قمر) begins with a moon letter. Easy to remember!

The Joining Sign (Wasla / Hamzat al-Waṣl)

In the Qur'an you will see two forms of the definite article: الـ (with alif, at the start of a sentence) and ٱلـ (with the wasla sign, in the middle of speech). The wasla tells you to link the lām directly to the final vowel of the preceding word, without pronouncing a separate "a" sound before the lām.

Special Alternative Forms of Long ā in the Qur'an

The Egyptian Standard Edition of the Qur'an uses two special forms that you must recognise:

1. Short Alif (الألف الخنجرية): Instead of a full alif, a small vertical stroke is written above the letter to indicate a long ā vowel. Example: ٱلرَّحْمَٰن — this is common in divine names like Raḥmān.

2. Alif Maqṣūra (ألف مقصورة): A yāʾ without dots at the end of a word, indicating a long ā sound. Example: هُدًى (hudan — guidance) and رَمَى (ramā — he threw). When a suffix is added to such words, the yāʾ reverts to a full alif.

Tanwīn — Nunation (التنوين)

Tanwīn is the addition of an "n" sound to the final vowel of an indefinite noun or adjective. It is written by doubling the vowel sign:

ً (fatḥatān) -an sound Example: كِتَابًا (kitāban)
ٌ (ḍammatān) -un sound Example: كِتَابٌ (kitābun)
ٍ (kasratān) -in sound Example: كِتَابٍ (kitābin)
The -an tanwīn in the accusative is written on an alif that is added to the end of the word: كِتَابًا. This alif is not pronounced separately — it is purely a spelling convention. Words ending in tāʾ marbūṭa (ة) or alif do not add this alif.

Hamza (الهمزة) — The Glottal Stop

Hamza represents the glottal stop — the brief catch in the throat heard in English "uh-oh". Its full name is hamzat al-qaṭʿ. In writing, hamza can appear in several different forms, depending on the vowel context around it. This is one of the most complex aspects of Arabic orthography.

Historical Background (from Prof. Jones): In seventh-century Arabia, the hamza had dropped out of use in some dialects — including that of Quraysh, the Prophet's ﷺ tribe. Other dialects preserved it. Classical Arabic owed more to those that preserved it. The resulting spelling system — where a "seat" (كُرْسِيّ, kursiyy) indicates the presence of hamza — was a historical compromise that has remained fixed.

The Two Types of Hamza

1. Hamzat al-Qaṭʿ (همزة القطع) — "Cutting Hamza"

The true glottal stop. It is always pronounced, even when following a vowel. It appears on three possible seats:

• On Alif: أَ / إِ / آ
• On Wāw: ؤُ
• On Yāʾ (without dots): ئِ
• Floating (no seat): ءَ

The choice of seat follows detailed rules based on surrounding vowels.

2. Hamzat al-Waṣl (همزة الوصل) — "Joining Hamza"

This is a prosthetic hamza — not a true glottal stop, but a vowel-starter placed before a cluster that would otherwise begin with an unvowelled consonant.

It appears at the start of:
• The definite article: الـ
• Some verb forms: اِكْتُبْ
• Some nouns: اِسْم

When preceded by a vowel, the hamzat al-waṣl is swallowed — not pronounced. It is then written with the wasla sign (ٱ).

The rules for which seat hamza takes are based on the surrounding vowels. As a simplified guide:

Pronunciation Guide

Jones writes: "It is impossible to give a proper explanation of pronunciation by written description alone. Much can be learned from listening to recorded recitations of the Qur'an, particularly if these are not too highly stylised."

Recommended approach: Use the written descriptions below as a starting framework, but supplement your study with listening to authentic Qur'anic recitation by qualified reciters (Qurrāʾ). The ear is ultimately your most important teacher for Arabic pronunciation.

The Emphatic (Mufakhkhama) Consonants

Four consonants are "emphatic" — they are pronounced with the back of the tongue raised toward the palate, causing surrounding vowels to sound "darker" (like English "au" in "caught" rather than "a" in "cat"):

ص Ṣād Emphatic S — darkens surrounding vowels
ض Ḍād Emphatic D — the letter unique to Arabic
ط Ṭāʾ Emphatic T
ظ Ẓāʾ Emphatic DH (not Z — Jones is explicit about this)

Sounds Most Challenging for English Speakers

ʿAyn (ع): This is the most challenging Arabic sound for English speakers. It is a voiced pharyngeal fricative produced by constricting (squeezing) the muscles of the upper throat. There is no equivalent in English. Listen carefully to native speakers and imitate. It appears frequently in key Islamic vocabulary: عِلْم (ʿilm — knowledge), عَرَبِيّ (ʿarabī — Arabic).

Ghayn (غ): Like the French "r" in "Paris" — a gargling, uvular sound. Closer to English speakers than ʿayn.

Qāf (ق): A "k" produced at the very back of the throat (the uvula), not at the palate. English "k" is not the same.

Ḥāʾ (ح): A forceful, breathy exhalation from deep in the throat — much stronger than English "h". The contrast between ح (ḥāʾ) and هـ (hāʾ) is crucial and must be mastered.

Lesson One

Nouns and Adjectives

الأسماء والصفات
Arabic nouns and adjectives may be either definite or indefinite. This lesson covers the indefinite article (tanwīn), the definite article (al-), grammatical gender, and the use of adjectives — including the crucial rule that allows a non-verbal sentence to exist without any verb.

Indefinite Nouns — Tanwīn (التنوين)

In English, indefiniteness is shown by the article "a" or "an" — we say "a book", "a mercy", "a messenger". Arabic has no indefinite article whatsoever. Instead, the large majority of nouns and adjectives signal that they are indefinite by taking tanwīn — the addition of the sound "n" to the final vowel of the word.

Tanwīn is written by doubling the final vowel sign. In the nominative case (the one you will encounter first), this gives the ending -un (written as ٌ — two ḍammas):

أَجْرٌ ajrun a reward
عَذَابٌ ʿadhābun a punishment
كِتَابٌ kitābun a scripture / book
آيَةٌ āyatun a sign / verse
رَحْمَةٌ raḥmatun a mercy
قُرْآنٌ qurʾānun a recitation

Words That Do NOT Take Tanwīn

This is a critical point that beginners often overlook: a considerable number of Arabic words do not take tanwīn even when they are indefinite. You can only learn which words these are by experience — they will be marked in this course and in the vocabularies.

⚠️ The Golden Rule on Non-Tanwīn Words

It is impossible to add tanwīn to words shown as not taking it. Any attempt to do so is a grammatical error. Two common examples from the very first lesson are:

  • آخَرُ (ākharu) — other, another
  • أَوَّلُ (awwalu) — first

You will encounter many more such words. They belong to a grammatical category called "partially inflected nouns" (الممنوع من الصرف), which is explained fully in Lesson 3.

Why Tanwīn Exists — A Deeper Explanation

The "n" sound of tanwīn was present in proto-Semitic languages as a morpheme. In Arabic it became fixed as the standard marker of indefiniteness on fully declinable nouns and adjectives. It is a suffix, not a separate word. When you master reading the Qur'an, you will hear it clearly in every recitation: the sound "-un", "-an", or "-in" at the end of indefinite words.

In the recitation of the Qur'an (Tajweed), tanwīn follows specific rules of assimilation and pronunciation — particularly when followed by certain letters. This is part of the science of Tajweed, which complements your grammar study.

Making a Noun Definite

According to Jones, a noun may be made definite in one of two ways:

Method 1: By being preceded by the definite article الـ (al-)

Method 2: By being followed by a genitive of possession (iḍāfa) — explained in full in Lesson 7.

This lesson deals only with Method 1.

The Definite Article — الـ (al-)

The definite article is equivalent to English "the". Traditional Arabic grammar describes it as الـ (al-), but Jones explains the linguistic reality more precisely: the definite article is essentially just the letter lām (ل). Because Arabic phonology does not allow a word to begin with an unvowelled consonant, a fatḥa is added to produce "al-". This fatḥa, however, is not considered an integral part of the article — it disappears in connected speech.

The article is written inseparably — attached directly to the word it makes definite:

الْبَلَدُ
al-baladu — the town
Compares with: بَلَدٌ (baladun — a town)
الْعَذَابُ
al-ʿadhābu — the punishment
الْكِتَابُ الْمُبِينُ
al-kitābu l-mubīnu — the clear scripture [Qur'an 12:1]
Notice: both noun AND adjective take the article

Sun Letter Assimilation — A Crucial Rule

When the definite article precedes a sun letter, the lām of the article is assimilated into that letter — meaning the lām changes its pronunciation to match the following consonant. A shadda is written over the sun letter to show the doubling.

The correct pronunciation is with the assimilated form (given in parentheses below), even though transliteration conventionally keeps writing "al-":

When the article precedes a word beginning with lām (ل), the Qur'anic text writes only one lām with a shadda: اللَّيْلُ (al-laylu). This is not the case in modern Arabic, where you would write اللَّيْل — both lāms shown. The Qur'anic convention is important to know.

The Critical Rule: Article vs. Tanwīn

📌 Fundamental Rule — Never Forget This

A word that has the definite article cannot also have tanwīn. A word cannot be definite and indefinite at the same time. This means:

✅ كِتَابٌ (kitābun) — a book (indefinite, with tanwīn)
✅ الْكِتَابُ (al-kitābu) — the book (definite, with article, NO tanwīn)
❌ الْكِتَابُنْ — IMPOSSIBLE. Never write or say this.

The Word اللَّهُ (Allāhu) — God

Jones explains the etymology: اللَّهُ (Allāhu) is a combination of the definite article and the word إِلَهٌ (ilāhun — a god), with the initial hamza of the noun dropped. Literally it means "The God" — i.e., the one, unique Deity. In some early Qur'anic manuscripts it was written without indicating the long vowel.

The word اللَّه has a special phonological rule: when preceded by a fatḥa or ḍamma, the lām is pronounced with "tafkhīm" (emphatic heaviness): Allāhu. When preceded by a kasra, the lām is light: lillāhi (as in بِسْمِ اللَّهِ). This is a Tajweed rule relevant to Qur'anic recitation.

Grammatical Gender in Arabic

Arabic has two grammatical genders: masculine (مذكر) and feminine (مؤنث). Every noun in Arabic is either masculine or feminine — there is no neuter. Adjectives, verbs, and pronouns all must agree with the gender of their noun.

The Working Rule

Treat words as masculine unless you have a reason to treat them as feminine. As you proceed, you will find that words may be feminine because of form, meaning, category, or convention.

Sources of Femininity

A word may be feminine for one of the following reasons:

1. Feminine by Form — Tāʾ Marbūṭa (تاء مربوطة)

The most common feminine ending is ـَة (written as a hāʾ with two dots above it). This is called the tāʾ marbūṭa — the "tied tāʾ". It is pronounced as "-atun" (indefinite) or "-atu/-ata/-ati" (definite, depending on case). In a pause (at the end of a sentence), it is usually pronounced like "h" or silently.

رَحْمَةٌ raḥmatun a mercy (feminine by form)
آيَةٌ āyatun a sign / verse (feminine by form)
مَدِينَةٌ madīnatun a city (feminine by form)
جَنَّةٌ jannatun a garden / Paradise
Exception! خَلِيفَةٌ (khalīfatun — caliph, successor) ends in tāʾ marbūṭa but is masculine. This is the only common masculine word with this ending. It has the strict Qur'anic sense of "successor" or "viceroy".

2. Feminine by Meaning (Natural Gender)

Words that refer to female beings are feminine regardless of their form:

أُمٌّ ummun mother (naturally feminine — no tāʾ marbūṭa)
بِنْتٌ bintun daughter

3. Feminine by Convention

Some words are treated as feminine purely by grammatical convention, even though they have no feminine marker and do not refer to females:

أَرْضٌ arḍun earth (treated as feminine by convention)
شَمْسٌ shamsun sun (feminine by convention)
نَارٌ nārun fire (feminine by convention)
يَدٌ yadun hand (feminine by convention)

The Use of Adjectives — Agreement (المطابقة)

In Arabic, an adjective used attributively (describing a noun) must follow the noun and agree with it in four things:

١ Definiteness Both definite, or both indefinite
٢ Gender Both masculine, or both feminine
٣ Number Singular, dual, or plural (Lesson 2 & 3)
٤ Case Nominative, accusative, or genitive (Lesson 3)

At this stage of Lesson 1, you can work with definiteness and gender. Number and case will be added in Lessons 2 and 3.

Multiple Adjectives on One Noun

A noun may take more than one adjective. In English, "and" is needed between adjectives in most cases: "God is powerful and mighty". In Arabic, adjectives can follow one another without any conjunction — although the particle وَ (wa- — "and") may be added:

اللَّهُ قَوِيٌّ عَزِيزٌ
Allāhu qawiyyun ʿazīzun
God is powerful and mighty
[Qur'an 58:21]
Two adjectives, no "and" needed in Arabic.
Single-letter words in Arabic, like وَ (wa- — "and"), cannot stand alone. They are always written as a prefix attached to the following word. This is why you see وَقَوِيٌّ not وَ قَوِيٌّ.

Simple Non-Verbal Sentences (الجمل الاسمية)

This is one of the most important and distinctive features of Arabic grammar. In English, every sentence needs a verb — including the verb "to be" in the present tense: "God IS mighty". In Arabic, the verb "to be" (كَانَ) is not used in the present tense. A complete sentence can consist of just two parts:

The Rule for Non-Verbal Sentences

A definite noun + an indefinite adjective = a complete sentence. The definite noun is the subject (المبتدأ — mubtadaʾ), and the indefinite adjective is the predicate (الخبر — khabar). No verb is needed.

اللَّهُ عَزِيزٌ
Allāhu ʿazīzun
God is mighty.
Subject (definite): اللَّهُ | Predicate (indefinite): عَزِيزٌ
اللَّهُ قَوِيٌّ
Allāhu qawiyyun
God is strong.
[Qur'an 8:52]
اللَّهُ قَوِيٌّ عَزِيزٌ
Allāhu qawiyyun ʿazīzun
God is powerful and mighty.
[Qur'an 58:21] — Two predicates, still no verb.
الرَّسُولُ أَمِينٌ
al-rasūlu amīnun
The messenger is trustworthy.

Why the Adjective Must Be Indefinite

This is the critical point: if both the noun and the adjective are definite, they form a noun phrase, not a sentence. Compare:

ArabicTypeEnglish
الْفَوْزُ الْعَظِيمُ Noun Phrase (both definite) the great victory (just a phrase, not a sentence)
الْفَوْزُ عَظِيمٌ Complete Sentence (definite + indefinite) The victory IS great. (a complete sentence)
اللَّهُ عَزِيزٌ Complete Sentence God is mighty.
This rule — that definiteness determines whether you have a phrase or a sentence — is the central principle of the Arabic nominal sentence. Mastering it early will allow you to read complex Qur'anic verses correctly.

Plurals

الجموع
Arabic has two types of plural: sound plurals (formed by endings) and broken plurals (formed by internal vowel change). Broken plurals are far more common in general Arabic, though the Qur'an has a higher proportion of sound plurals than later Arabic. One critical grammar rule governs all broken plurals — and it is a rule that beginners consistently find surprising.

Sound Plurals (الجمع السالم)

Sound plurals are formed by adding an ending to the singular — the root of the word remains intact (hence "sound"). There are two types: masculine and feminine.

Sound Masculine Plural (جمع المذكر السالم)

Formed by adding ـُونَ (-ūna) in the nominative, or ـِينَ (-īna) in the accusative and genitive. The singular must lose its tanwīn.

Restriction on Sound Masculine Plural

The masculine sound plural is very restricted in use. Jones identifies only three common categories:
1. Active participles (like ظَالِمٌ "wrongdoer" → ظَالِمُونَ)
2. Words of the tawwābun pattern (intensive meaning — professions/attributes, like تَوَّابٌ "relenting" → تَوَّابُونَ)
3. Relative adjectives (like عَجَمِيٌّ "foreign" → عَجَمِيُّونَ)

SingularPlural (Nom.)Plural (Acc./Gen.)Meaning
ظَالِمٌظَالِمُونَظَالِمِينَwrongdoer(s)
تَوَّابٌتَوَّابُونَتَوَّابِينَrelenting (one/ones)
مُؤْمِنٌمُؤْمِنُونَمُؤْمِنِينَbeliever(s)
مُسْلِمٌمُسْلِمُونَمُسْلِمِينَMuslim(s)
كَافِرٌكَافِرُونَكَافِرِينَunbeliever(s)
A handful of Qur'anic words are feminine singulars ending in -atun but take a sound masculine plural. The most important is سَنَةٌ (sanatun — a year), plural سِنُونَ / سِنِينَ (sinūna/sinīna). (Modern Arabic has replaced this with the sound feminine plural.)

Sound Feminine Plural (جمع المؤنث السالم)

Formed by replacing the tāʾ marbūṭa (ة) with ـَاتٌ (-ātun) in the nominative, or ـَاتٍ (-ātin) in the accusative and genitive.

Important Caution

You cannot assume that all words ending in ة take a sound feminine plural. Many do not. Conversely, some words that do NOT end in ة take a sound feminine plural, e.g.: سَمَاءٌ (samāʾun — sky/heaven) → سَمَاوَاتٌ (samāwātun).

SingularPlural (Nom.)Plural (Acc./Gen.)Meaning
مُؤْمِنَةٌمُؤْمِنَاتٌمُؤْمِنَاتٍbelieving woman/women
آيَةٌآيَاتٌآيَاتٍsign(s), verse(s)
سَمَاءٌسَمَاوَاتٌسَمَاوَاتٍheaven(s) — IRREGULAR!

Broken Plurals (الجمع التكسيري)

Broken plurals are formed by a change of vowel pattern within the word — the root consonants stay the same but the vowels change, sometimes drastically. This is analogous to English mouse→mice, foot→feet, man→men. The vast majority of Arabic nouns use broken plurals.

Key reality for beginners: At the outset, it is impossible to predict what broken plural form a noun will take. You must learn each noun's plural alongside the singular from the very beginning. The vocabularies in this course always give the plural — learn them together.

The Most Important Rule About Broken Plurals

This is one of the most surprising rules for English speakers, and one that Jones emphasises strongly. Master it now and it will save you from countless errors:

📌 THE GOLDEN RULE OF BROKEN PLURALS

Broken plurals are treated grammatically as FEMININE SINGULARS — unless they refer specifically to male persons (in which case they take masculine plural agreement) or female persons (feminine plural agreement).

This means: when a broken plural noun does NOT refer to male persons, any adjective agreeing with it must be in the FEMININE SINGULAR form.

Correct Examples

The word قَوْمٌ is singular in form but collective in meaning ("people, folk"). It normally takes plural adjectives and verbs, even though it is grammatically singular:

⚠️ A Very Common Beginner Mistake

Students often try to add a feminine ending to a broken plural adjective — e.g., writing كُتُبٌ قَيِّمَاتٌ instead of كُتُبٌ قَيِّمَةٌ. Jones states this is "totally impossible". The feminine singular is the correct form for non-person broken plurals. The plural ending on the adjective is simply WRONG.

Broken Plural Adjectives

Jones notes: some broken plural nouns (not referring to persons) may also take broken plural adjectives. An example from the Qur'an: أَيَّامٌ أُخَرُ (ayyāmun ukharu — "other days") [2:184]. However, feminine plural adjectives with non-person broken plurals, though occasionally found in early Qur'anic usage, became increasingly rare over time and should not be used.

The Declension of Nouns

إعراب الأسماء
Arabic has three grammatical cases — nominative, accusative, and genitive — each signalled by different vowel endings. Understanding declension is essential for reading the Qur'an accurately and for understanding grammatical relationships within sentences. This lesson sets out all five types of declension in Arabic, along with the sound plural declensions.

The Three Grammatical Cases

Arabic has three cases. The case of a noun is shown by the vowel (or vowel + tanwīn) on its final letter. Jones gives a slightly simplified but completely functional explanation of their uses:

الرَّفْعُ Nominative (Rafʿ) Used for the subject (in most places) and for some predicates. Ending: -u / -un
الْجَرُّ Genitive (Jarr) Used after prepositions and to denote possession (iḍāfa). Ending: -i / -in
النَّصْبُ Accusative (Naṣb) Used if you cannot use nominative or genitive. Ending: -a / -an
Jones's Practical Rule for the Accusative

"The accusative is used if you cannot use the nominative or genitive." This apparently simple rule actually covers a great deal of Arabic grammar. The direct object of a verb, for instance, is accusative — because it is neither subject nor following a preposition. Many other uses will become clear as lessons progress.

Up to and including Lesson 2, all examples have been in the nominative. Lesson 4 introduces the genitive (after prepositions). Lesson 8 introduces the accusative (as the object of verbs).

Why learn the declension tables? Jones explains: "Learning the types in this way is not the theoretical exercise it might seem to be. It helps to give structure to one's knowledge, and, as with the verb tables that come later, it is akin to learning multiplication tables for arithmetic."

Type One — Words with Tāʾ Marbūṭa (ة)

Type One consists of words that have the feminine ending ة. These are the simplest forms to decline. There are two sub-types:

Type One a) — Fully Inflected (3 case endings)

Most feminine nouns fall here. They have three distinct case endings:

Proper nouns ending in ة (like مَكَّةُ, Makkah) are partially inflected. In the indefinite they have only two distinct endings (nominative -u, but accusative and genitive both -a, with no tanwīn). However, since proper nouns are by definition definite, any adjective attached to them must have the definite article.

These include: the elative (comparative/superlative) forms like أَسْوَدُ (aswadu — black/blacker), certain proper nouns, and other specific word patterns. When indefinite they have only two distinct endings (nominative -u, accusative and genitive both -a, no tanwīn). When definite, they decline like fully inflected words.

Lesson Four

Prepositions

حروف الجر
Prepositions are among the most frequently used words in the Qur'an. Every preposition in Arabic puts the following noun into the genitive case. This lesson introduces all the major prepositions, their meanings, their combinations with the definite article, and the concept of the prepositional phrase as a sentence predicate.

Prepositions and the Genitive Case

Jones states the governing rule at the outset: all prepositions in Arabic govern the genitive case. This means every noun that follows a preposition must take the genitive ending (-i/-in for fully inflected nouns, or -a for partially inflected ones).

The Genitive Rule

Whenever a noun follows a preposition — whether a single-letter preposition attached directly to the noun, or a multi-letter one written separately — that noun is in the genitive case.

Fully inflected: -in (indefinite) or -i (definite)
Sound feminine plural: -ātin (indefinite) or -āti (definite)
Sound masculine plural: -īna (indefinite or definite)

فِي الْأَرْضِ
fī l-arḍi — in the earthPreposition فِي + definite noun in genitive (-i ending)
مِنَ السَّمَاءِ
mina s-samāʾi — from the sky/heavenPreposition مِنْ + definite noun in genitive
لِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ
li-l-muʾminīna — for the believersPreposition لِ attached + definite sound m. plural in genitive (-īna)

Single-Letter Prepositions (Attached Directly to the Noun)

Three prepositions in Arabic are single letters, written attached inseparably to the beginning of the following word — exactly like the definite article:

1. بِـ (bi-) — with, by, by means of, in (an oath)

This preposition has a wide range of meanings. It can indicate instrument ("by means of"), accompaniment ("with"), or be used in oaths. When attached before the definite article, the alif of the article drops and only the lām remains:

بِاللَّهِ bi-llāhi by God! (oath) / with/through God
بِالْحَقِّ bi-l-ḥaqqi with the truth / in truth
بِكِتَابٍ bi-kitābin with a book / by a scripture
بِسْمِ اللَّهِ bismi llāhi In the name of God (Basmalah)
The Basmalah (بِسْمِ اللَّهِ الرَّحْمَنِ الرَّحِيمِ) begins every sūra of the Qur'an except Sūra 9. It uses the preposition بِـ: bismi llāhi r-raḥmāni r-raḥīmi — "In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Most Merciful." Note the assimilation of both occurrences of al- before the sun letter rāʾ.

2. لِـ (li-) — for, to, belonging to, in order to

This preposition indicates purpose, direction, or possession/attribution. Before the definite article it becomes لِلـ (li-l-). Note: before اللَّهِ specifically, it becomes لِلَّهِ (li-llāhi).

لِلَّهِ li-llāhi to/for/belonging to God
لِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ li-l-muʾminīna for the believers
لِلنَّاسِ li-n-nāsi for people (sun letter assimilation)
لِكُلِّ قَوْمٍ li-kulli qawmin for every people
Special case — لَهُ / لَكَ: When لِ is followed by a pronoun suffix, the kasra changes to a fatḥa: لَهُ (lahu — for him), لَكَ (laka — for you), لَهُمْ (lahum — for them). But لِي (lī — for me) keeps the kasra.

3. كـ (ka-) — like, as

This preposition is used for comparison. It is less frequent than بِ and لِ:

كَالْجِبَالِ ka-l-jibāli like mountains
كَمِثْلِهِ ka-mithlihi like His like / like unto Him

The Preposition مِنْ (min) — Special Uses

Jones devotes particular attention to مِنْ because it has such a wide range of uses in the Qur'an. The most important:

Uses of مِنْ (min)
  • Origin/source: مِنَ الْجَنَّةِ (from Paradise)
  • Partitive (some of): مِنَ النَّاسِ (some of the people)
  • In comparisons (than): خَيْرٌ مِنْهُ (better than it)
  • Explaining what something consists of: مِنْ نُورٍ (of/from light)
  • Separation (away from): بَعِيدٌ مِنَ الظَّالِمِينَ (far from the wrongdoers)

مِنْ and إِلَى Before the Definite Article

When مِنْ or عَنْ comes before a word with the definite article (ال), the final sukūn of the preposition changes to a fatḥa for ease of pronunciation:

مِنَ النَّاسِ
mina n-nāsi — from/among the peopleمِنْ → مِنَ before the article
عَنِ الْقَوْمِ
ʿani l-qawmi — from/concerning the peopleعَنْ → عَنِ before the article

Prepositional Phrases as Sentence Predicates

In Lesson 1 we saw that an indefinite adjective can serve as the predicate of a sentence when following a definite noun (اللَّهُ عَزِيزٌ — God is mighty). Jones now introduces a related construction: a prepositional phrase can similarly serve as the predicate of a non-verbal sentence.

Prepositional Phrase as Predicate

A definite subject + a prepositional phrase = a complete sentence.
The prepositional phrase acts as a predicate, giving a locative meaning ("X is in/at/from Y").

اللَّهُ فِي السَّمَاءِ
Allāhu fī s-samāʾi — God is in the heavenDefinite subject + prepositional phrase = complete sentence (no verb)
الْمُؤْمِنُونَ مَعَ اللَّهِ
al-muʾminūna maʿa llāhi — the believers are with God
الْحَقُّ مِنْ رَبِّكَ
al-ḥaqqu min rabbika — the truth is from your Lord[3:60] — Note: rabbika uses the suffix -ka (your, m.s.) — covered in Lesson 5

The Word كُلُّ (kullu — all, every)

Jones introduces this word here because it frequently appears with prepositions. كُلُّ is always the first of an iḍāfa construction (see Lesson 7). Its meaning depends on what follows:

كُلُّ شَيْءٍ kullu shayʾin every thing / all things
كُلُّ النَّاسِ kullu n-nāsi all the people
كُلُّهُمْ kulluhum all of them
لِكُلِّ قَوْمٍ li-kulli qawmin for every people
